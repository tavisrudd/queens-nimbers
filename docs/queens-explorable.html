The Queens Game play it, then see inside the solver

Two players take turns placing queens on an n×n board so that no queen attacks another. If it is your turn and every remaining square is attacked, you lose. Simple to state — but on a 16×16 board deciding the winner took a ten-billion-node search, and on 18×18 the answer (the first player wins, opening at the centre) is a new result.

This page is the interactive companion to the narrative report and the formal paper. Everything here runs inside this single file: the boards below are driven by a real solver — the same rules, symmetry folding, memoization, and move ordering as the Rust engine, ported to JavaScript and cross-checked against its known values.

2Odd boards are free

On any odd board the first player wins with zero search: take the centre, then answer every one of your moves with its 180° rotation. Try to survive — the computer never has to think, and it always has a reply.

Board
—

The invariantAfter the centre move, the set of free squares is exactly 180°-symmetric — and it stays that way after every mirrored reply.

Why it worksA square attacks its own 180° image only from the centre lines, and the centre is already taken — so whenever you find a free square, its mirror is also free after your move. The computer can always answer; you run out first. This pairing argument is why every odd n is a first-player win, no computer needed — the hard, open cases are the even boards. Report §3.

The scaling law There is no fixed base: each two extra rows multiply the searched positions by a growing factor — run the measurement to see the sequence. Odd boards stay cheap (the centre-and-mirror win of scene 2 shows up as a sawtooth); even boards must refute every first move, and that is where the ten-billion-node searches come from. Memoization + symmetry is what keeps the curve on this chart at all. Report §2, §7.
